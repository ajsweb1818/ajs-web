Focus

AJS publishes research that expands our knowledge of the Earth, with an emphasis on the study of the structure, composition, and evolution of our planet, and the physical, chemical, and biological processes that have influenced its evolution. Recent papers have focused on geochemistry, geochronology, geodynamics, geomorphology, basin analysis, Earth history, petrology, tectonics, and thermochronology, but new directions are welcomed.

Publication Options

The publication options below are distinguished, in part, by length, which AJS measures in publication units (PU). Briefly, each 500-word increment of the main text counts for 1 PU, and each figure and table counts for 1.5 PU. The term free limit indicates the maximum PU length that will be published free of charge.

Evaluation Criteria

AJS seeks papers that rank high in terms of the following criteria, after Larsson and Patriksson (2016). Please consider these criteria as you write your paper.

  1. Relevance: How does the paper relate to current scientific imperatives?
  2. Generality: What is the reach of the paper in terms of research focus and results?
  3. Originality: Is the research unique, creative, or innovative?
  4. Progress: Does the paper provide a significant advance in scientific knowledge?
  5. Accessibility: Is the paper accessible to a broad audience?
  6. Scientific Foundation: Does the paper establish a clear foundation based on accepted theory and/or methodology? Is that foundation clearly described and referenced at the beginning of the paper?
  7. Integration: Does the paper provide a clear integration of previous knowledge with new results? Does it make connections with research in other fields, or indicate a promising approach for future research?
  8. Consistency: Are the results and conclusions consistent with the claims and ambitions stated in the introduction?
  9. News Value: Is the paper of interest to the broader scientific community or to the general public?

Web-Based Submission

All manuscripts must be submitted via the AJS Manuscript Manager system. The submission tool includes the following questions, which are important for the initial evaluation by the Editorial Board.

Submit through Manuscript Manager
  1. Overview: A brief description of the essential research question addressed by the paper, and the originality and significance of the paper.
  2. Authors: A list of authors, with their affiliations and ORCID numbers.
  3. Statement of Original Content: A statement indicating that the content is original and that the paper is not in review, in press, or already published elsewhere. Any overlap with other publications must be discussed. AJS may reject or retract papers if significant undisclosed overlap is discovered after submission or publication.
  4. Reviewers: A list of five or more potential reviewers, with a brief statement for each indicating their expertise relative to the paper. Authors may also identify reviewers they feel should be avoided, with a brief explanation for each. AJS cannot guarantee that these suggestions will be followed, but the information is often helpful.
  5. Editors and Associate Editors: Authors may suggest AJS Editors and Associate Editors who are well suited to handle the paper.

Authorship

At AJS, the lead author is generally the person who has performed most of the work and taken the lead in writing the paper. Coauthors are those who have made significant scientific contributions and are generally listed in descending order of contribution. The paper must include an accurate statement describing the contribution of each listed author. All coauthors must agree to the submission. Upon submission, AJS sends an inquiry to each coauthor to verify that all authors agree with the decision to submit.

Correspondence during review, revision, and publication will be directed to the corresponding author. The lead author usually serves in this role, but another author may be designated when appropriate. Authors identify the corresponding author during the submission process.

Paper Length

AJS uses publication units (PU) to measure the length of a digital publication. This measure sets limits for short-form publications and defines a free limit for long-form publications. An excess length fee of $100 per excess PU applies when a long-form publication exceeds the free limit.

  1. Text content: 1 PU per 500 words. The count includes the main body, equations, appendices, and references. The title, author list and affiliations, keywords, and figure and table captions are excluded.
  2. Figures and tables: Add 1.5 PU for each figure and each table.
  3. Rounding: Round the total PU up to the next whole number.
  4. Equations: If a manuscript contains equations, the first group of up to 10 equations counts as 1 PU. Each additional group of up to 10 equations is counted as 1 excess PU, regardless of whether the manuscript exceeds the free publication-unit limit.
Base PU = number of words / 500 + (number of figures + number of tables) × 1.5

Round the base PU up to the next whole number. A manuscript with no equations receives no equation PU. If a manuscript contains 1–10 equations, those equations add 1 PU. Each additional group of up to 10 equations is charged as 1 excess PU, even when the manuscript otherwise remains within the applicable free limit.

Note: Appendices are considered part of the manuscript and are included in the publication-unit calculation. Material submitted as Supplementary Information is published separately and is not included in the publication-unit calculation.

Authors are encouraged to include only those equations that are essential for understanding the main text. Extended mathematical derivations, detailed calculations, and other supporting material may be provided as Supplementary Information when appropriate. This approach helps keep the main article concise while minimizing publication costs.

Examples

Example 1: A Science Paper with 15,000 words and 10 figures and tables combined has a base length of 45 PU, which is the free limit for a Science Paper.

Example 2: A Science Paper with 20,000 words and 12 figures and tables combined has a base length of 58 PU, resulting in 13 excess PU and an excess-length fee of $1,300.

Example 3: An article with 8,000 words, 4 figures, and 32 equations has a base length of 23 PU (16 PU for text, 6 PU for figures, and 1 PU for the first 10 equations). The remaining 22 equations are charged as three excess PU (equations 11–20, 21–30, and 31–32), resulting in an excess-equation fee of $300.

Statistical Terms

A population is a set of similar items or events of interest for a question or experiment. A sample is a subset of those items or events and is used to represent the statistical properties of the population. Those properties are characterized by statistical parameters. A common convention is to use Greek letters for population parameters, such as population mean μ and population standard deviation σ, and Roman letters for parameters estimated from samples, such as sample mean m and sample standard deviation s.

Population parameters are generally unknown, and sample parameters are estimated. The uncertainty of the estimated mean is usually called the standard error, or the standard deviation of the sample mean, and is labeled SE or sm, where SE = s/√n and n is the sample size.

Recommendations for Reporting Errors

The following guidelines are provided to ensure clearer reporting of errors and uncertainties.

  1. Errors should not be cited without identifying the method and level of the estimate. The two most common methods are SE, the standard error (or standard deviation of the mean), and CI, a confidence interval. The SE method is more flexible because it does not require a specific probability distribution. The CI method may be more precise, but only when the probability distribution is known. Typical levels include 1SE, 2SE, 68% CI, and 98% CI.
  2. Report errors consistently throughout the paper. In that case, the method and level need to be stated only once near the beginning, for example: “All errors in this paper are reported as 2SE unless noted otherwise.”
  3. Do not confuse SE with SD, the sample-based standard deviation. SD estimates the dispersion of the sample data; SE estimates the expected dispersion of the sample mean, where SE = SD/√n.
  4. In some cases, an error is better represented by SD than SE. For example, for n = 100 detrital grain ages with mean = 50 Ma and SD = 5 Ma, the precision of the estimated mean is SE = SD/√n = 0.5 Ma. If the focus is instead the expected range of a newly selected grain age from the same distribution, SD = 5 Ma is the more appropriate representation.
  5. AJS advises against using 1σ, 2σ, and similar notation for the level of an error estimate because σ is widely used for the population standard deviation, not the standard error. This notation may lead readers to question whether SD and SE have been confused.

Preparing Your Manuscript for Submission

The initial manuscript must be submitted as a Microsoft Word or PDF file. Use 12-point Times New Roman with 1.5 line spacing (approximately four lines per inch). Continuous line numbering must be included throughout the manuscript to facilitate peer review. AJS will not begin evaluation or review until a line-numbered manuscript is received.

A submitted manuscript may not exceed 30 MB. Reduce the combined file size if the submission exceeds this limit. This limit applies only during review. After acceptance, AJS will coordinate with authors as needed to accommodate large files and preserve full-resolution figures and other content.

The manuscript should contain the following parts in the order shown below.

Title

A title should be specific, informative, and brief. Avoid abbreviations and acronyms; if they are used, define them in the abstract.

Author List

List the authors after the title, followed by their affiliations or addresses. Use numbered footnotes to link each author to an affiliation. The affiliation should indicate where the research was performed. An author may include a second footnote for a current affiliation.

Marie Curie1,2
1University of Paris, 2Institut du Radium

Indicate the corresponding author with an asterisk in the author list and provide the email address in a footnote.

Corresponding author: charles.darwin@beagle.org

Abstract

The abstract is a concise, freestanding summary of fewer than 300 words. Define all abbreviations within the abstract. Do not refer to external information such as references, tables, or figures.

Keywords

Provide at least three keywords that summarize the topic, content, and/or focus of the paper. Authors may design their own keywords or use terms in common usage, such as USGS keywords.

Main Text

Sections

The main text should be divided into numbered sections, each with its own heading. Use Arabic numerals. Three section levels are allowed.

1. INTRODUCTION
1.1. Basic Ideas
1.1.1. Definition of an idea

Footnotes

Footnote comments are allowed in the main text but should be used sparingly.

Citations

AJS uses different styles and rules for citations and references to published and unpublished sources. For published sources, AJS follows the Publication Manual of the American Psychological Association, 7th edition (2020).

For AJS purposes, a published source is content that is publicly accessible in print or digital form. Examples include journal articles, books, theses, dissertations, databases, software releases, and technical reports, provided they are publicly available. Publication does not guarantee quality or reliability, but it allows others to evaluate the source independently.

Each citation to a published source must have a full matching reference in the References section. Unpublished sources may be cited in the text, but they are not included in the References section.

Equations

Center equations on the page and number them sequentially, with the numbers in parentheses at the right margin. Refer to them in the text as equation (1), or parenthetically as (eq 1) or (eqs 14 and 15). Capitalize “Equation” when it begins a sentence, as in “Equation (1).”

For initial submission, equations may be prepared by any method, including Microsoft Word or LaTeX. For publication, the manuscript and equations must be provided in files created with a current version of Microsoft Word.

Tables

Submit each table as a single file. Prepare tables with the Table function in Microsoft Word. Number tables sequentially according to their placement in the paper. Place the table number and a brief descriptive title above the table, for example: “Table 1. Friction coefficient for sliding.”

Use only separate cells, ordered lists, or unordered lists to separate content within a cell into individual lines. Do not use spaces, tabs, HTML tags, or manual line breaks inside a table. Symbols indicating statistical significance should appear in the same cell as the value and should not occupy a separate column.

Individual values may be highlighted with boldface, italics, or shading. Use only one type of shading and one font size. Underlining is not permitted, and text color is limited to black. Any shading will be standardized by the publisher.

In the text, refer to each table using the lowercase word “table” followed by its number, as in “table 1” or “(table 1).” Capitalize the word when it begins a sentence: “Table 1.”

Figures

Prepare figures in JPEG, TIFF, or PNG format. Vector formats, including EPS and PDF, are not allowed. Raster resolution should be at least 300 dpi to ensure sufficient quality in the published paper.

Submit each figure as a single file, including composite figures with multiple parts. Each figure must fit within the print limits of a single published page: 17.25 × 25.5 cm (6.8 × 10.0 inches). Larger figures may be included as Supplementary Information. Label the parts of a composite figure with successive uppercase letters: A, B, C, and so forth.

Number figures sequentially according to their placement in the paper. In the text, use the lowercase word “figure” followed by its number, as in “figure 1.” Capitalize the word at the beginning of a sentence: “Figure 1.” Use the abbreviation in parentheses, as in “(fig. 1),” and at the start of a figure caption, as in “Fig. 1. Plots showing Y versus Zr.” For parts of a composite figure, use forms such as “figure 1A” or “(fig. 3A and D).”

Authors are responsible for obtaining permission to reproduce previously published figures. The permission must be acknowledged in the figure caption.

Acknowledgments

This section should identify funding sources and thank colleagues, other contributors, and reviewers.

Author Contributions

Provide a brief summary of each author’s contribution to the paper.

Data and Supplementary Information

Report the nature and location of data and supplementary information that are essential to the paper but too large to include in the main article. Supplementary Information may include extended mathematical derivations, additional figures or tables, detailed methods, datasets, computer code, numerical-experiment results, or other supporting material that is not essential to the main narrative.

Supplementary Information is published separately from the main article and is not included in the publication-unit calculation. Authors may use it, when scientifically appropriate, to keep the main article focused and to avoid unnecessary publication charges.

Authors are encouraged to archive databases, computer code, and numerical-experiment results as needed to support the paper. Data and software citations must also appear in the References section.

AJS maintains an online repository for data and supplementary information. Zenodo also provides an open-access repository suitable for computer code, numerical results, and datasets. Zenodo supplies DOI links, making archived contributions easy to cite in the References section.

References

All published sources cited in the paper must be fully referenced in this section. Citations to unpublished sources are excluded. References must follow APA 7th edition style. Each reference should include all authors, publication year, full title, source, and DOI link when available. Format DOI links as https://doi.org/xxxxx.

Manning, C. E., & Bird, D. K. (1991). Porosity evolution and fluid flow in the basalts of the Skaergaard magma-hydrothermal system, east Greenland. American Journal of Science, 291(3), 201–257. https://doi.org/10.2475/ajs.291.3.201

Hox, J. J., Moerbeek, M., & van de Schoot, R. (2017). Multilevel analysis: Techniques and applications (3rd ed.). Routledge.
